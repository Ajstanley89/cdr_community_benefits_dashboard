Here are some commonly asked questions about Direct Air Capture with Storage (DACS)

DACS is a technology designed to remove carbon dioxide (CO₂) directly from the atmosphere. The captured CO₂ can either be stored underground in geological formations or utilized in industrial processes, such as creating synthetic fuels or building materials.

While DACS is designed to mitigate climate change, it can pose certain environmental risks, including:
  • Energy demand: DACS facilities require significant energy, which, if sourced from fossil fuels, could negate some of the climate benefits.
  • Land use: Large-scale DACS facilities may require substantial land, potentially disrupting ecosystems or competing with agricultural and urban needs.
  • Chemical waste: The chemical sorbents used to capture CO₂ (e.g., amines) could lead to waste or leakage if not properly managed.
  • Water usage: Some DACS processes consume large amounts of water, which could strain water resources in arid regions.
  • Storage risks: Improper CO₂ storage in geological formations could lead to leakage, potentially harming local environments or communities.

To reduce risks, DACS facilities can:
  • Use renewable energy to power operations, minimizing emissions from energy use.
  • Implement rigorous monitoring of CO2 storage sites to prevent leakage.
  • Develop sustainable sorbents with lower toxicity and waste production.
  • Design facilities to minimize land and water usage, prioritizing areas with minimal ecological impact.

Positive Impacts:
  • Job creation: DACS facilities require skilled workers for construction, operation, and maintenance, creating jobs in engineering, chemistry, and project management.
  • New industries: The growth of DACS can spur related industries, such as CO₂ utilization (e.g., synthetic fuels, concrete production).
  • Economic diversification: Regions reliant on fossil fuels can transition workers to DACS-related roles, supporting a green economy.
Negative Impacts:
  • Displacement: Workers in traditional fossil fuel industries may face job losses as DACS and other green technologies grow.
  • Skill gaps: Transitioning to DACS may require retraining workers, which can be challenging without proper programs and funding.
  • Unequal distribution: Job opportunities may be concentrated in certain regions, leaving others behind.

DACS can contribute to a sustainable job market by:
  • Supporting the transition to a low-carbon economy.
  • Creating long-term roles in facility operation, maintenance, and research.
  • Encouraging innovation in CO2 utilization industries, which can generate additional employment opportunities.
  • Providing a pathway for fossil fuel workers to transition to green jobs.
